Burkina Faso's Sovereign Gold Strategy
Joe Dusevic
Independent graduate research paper · 16 October 2026
Abstract
Since 2022, Burkina F aso's military government has extended state control over much of
the country's formal gold trade, while leaving its largest foreign-run mines in private hands
and staying in the CF A franc it denounces. This paper examines that pattern as Burkina
F aso's Sovereign Gold Strategy: a configuration of policies through which the state
extends control over the production, circulation and rents of gold without owning the
whole sector . The strategy is defined by the interplay of four properties: protracted
counter-insurgency , pragmatic state capitalism, systemic monetary exposure and
rhetorical dissonance, the regime's public denunciation of a currency whose benefits its
rhetoric does not acknowledge. Using W est African Resources' audited accounts, the
paper estimates what the strategy's instruments yield and identifies a control–capacity
squeeze: a structural vulnerability in which control creates commitments that may
outgrow the state's capacity to finance, operate and secure them. On an exploratory
measure against the gold income the fiscal rule leaves free, the squeeze would bind after
a f all in the gold price of about a fifth, and sooner with an oil shock or higher war spending.
A comparison with Mali and Niger shows that the strategy does not explain why Burkina
F aso keeps the franc, though it raises the cost of leaving. The paper then applies the
framework to a f alling gold price, the turn to Russia and Australian investment, recasting
the risk to W est African Resources as a gradual transfer of value and an asymmetric
exposure to gold rather than nationalisation.

The Sahel's military governments have broken with France in almost every way but one.

States (AES), has proposed a gold-backed currency , the sira, but it exists only on paper .

2. Defining the strategy
Burkina F aso's Sovereign Gold Strategy aims to expand effective state control over the
production, circulation and rents of gold without ownership of the whole sector . What
distinguishes it from simple state ownership or a tax regime is the way four properties
specific to Burkina F aso's situation act on one another .
2.1 The four properties
Protracted counter-insurgency creates the pressure for control and limits its reach. Gold
finances armed groups, so controlling it becomes security policy , but a state that cannot
hold its mining areas must exercise control where it is strong. A state's capacity to decide
differs from its capacity to implement decisions across its territory (Mann, 1984), and
African states have long struggled to project power beyond their core (Herbst, 2000).
Pragmatic state capitalism supplies the instruments. The state acts as owner , investor and
trader rather than only as regulator (Bremmer , 2010), combining the roles of entrepreneur ,
majority investor and minority investor (Musacchio and Lazzarini, 2014). It extends control
along the gold chain without owning every stage, a form of quasi-vertical integration
(Blois, 1972) whose choices resemble a make-or-buy calculation (Williamson, 1985): it buys
where trade is informal, owns where assets f all to it and taxes where foreign operators are
profitable. Its leverage rises once investment is sunk (V ernon, 1971) and when prices are
high (Bremmer and Johnston, 2009), and it aims mainly to capture revenue rather than
build industry (Wilson, 2015).
Systemic monetary exposure constrains the choice among those instruments. Every
instrument depends on the gold price, and inside a currency union the state lacks the
authority to issue currency , set policy and manage the exchange rate (Cohen, 1998) that
might cushion a f all. Monetary sovereignty has historically served state-building
(Helleiner , 2003), but sharing a currency trades it for stability and credit (Mundell, 1961;
Alesina and Barro, 2002).
Rhetorical dissonance shapes the political setting. States routinely decouple what they
say about sovereignty from what they do (Krasner , 1999), and organisations f acing
conflicting demands often satisfy one audience with talk and another with action
(Brunsson, 1989). In Burkina F aso its core is the absence, in the regime's public rhetoric
examined here, of any acknowledgement of the benefits of the currency it denounces:
the rhetoric treats the CF A franc as a colonial chain, while the strategy depends on the
convertibility , credit and stability the franc provides. The first three properties are
structural conditions; the fourth is the political mechanism that lets the regime live with
them, and the evidence for it is accordingly more interpretive.

2.2 The interplay
The properties form a system rather than a list. The war makes gold control urgent and
pushes it downstream, to purchasing, refining and export control, the instruments that
work where the army holds ground; it also underpins the rhetoric, since a regime whose
legitimacy rests on defeating the insurgency frames gold control as part of the fight.
Those instruments deepen monetary exposure by committing the state to buying,
financing and taxing gold whose value it cannot control. That exposure, and the
convertibility and credit the instruments depend on, restrain the rhetoric's radical
implications, which is why the regime denounces the franc but stays in it. The rhetoric in
turn demands visible acts of control, pressing the state into stakes and state enterprises
that add to its commitments.
Remove any one property and the strategy changes. Without the war , gold control would
be a fiscal choice rather than a security imperative; without pragmatic state capitalism,
the state would lack the flexible mix of buying, owning and taxing that lets it match
instruments to conditions; without monetary exposure, it would have the exchange rate
as a further , if costly , means of absorbing a price f all; and without the dissonance, the
regime would have to act on its words or abandon them.
2.3 Why gold
Gold suits the strategy for three reasons. It can be captured from below: much of it is dug
by hand and sold informally , so the state can extend its reach by becoming the main
official buyer , as post-independence marketing boards did with smallholder crops,
extracting revenue and provoking smuggling as they went (Bates, 1981). It is fought over:
portable, valuable and often found where the state is weak. And it can be money . Y et
control of gold is not monetary sovereignty . The state must acquire gold, retain it rather
than sell it, and hold it as an audited reserve in an institution entitled to issue currency
against it. Burkina F aso has gone f ar on the first step; evidence on the second is thin; the
institutions for the third do not exist.
2.4 The control–capacity squeeze
The interplay creates a characteristic vulnerability , which the paper calls a control–
capacity squeeze: the condition in which a state's claims over a resource expand f aster
than its ability to finance, operate and secure them. Burkina F aso can decree a 15 per cent
stake or an export ban; it cannot police every pit, finance every acquisition or run every
mine. Unlike ordinary resource dependence, the squeeze arises because control itself
generates commitments: acquisitions to pay for , mines to run, counters and convoys to
protect, and a buying network to fund. Each property contributes: pragmatic state
capitalism creates the commitments, the war adds security obligations while limiting the
state's reach, monetary exposure limits the means to finance them, and the rhetoric

makes retreat politically costly . Pragmatic state capitalism is also the main safeguard,
since much of its pragmatism consists in avoiding commitments the state cannot meet:
taking over only mines whose owners are leaving, negotiating stakes rather than seizing
them, and taxing rather than running profitable operators. The squeeze is therefore most
likely to surf ace early where that restraint gives way , in state purchasing at scale. It need
not take the form of a state funding crisis: the state can shift costs onto miners and
traders through new levies, mandatory dividends or slower payment, displacing the
squeeze rather than resolving it. Its signs would be delayed payments or heavier
borrowing, lower buying prices or f alling collections, operating strain at state mines, and
new levies on foreign miners. The paper treats the squeeze as a structural vulnerability
rather than a demonstrated constraint: the commitments are observable, but whether
they already exceed the state's capacity cannot be established without the accounts of
SONASP and SOP AMIB.
The squeeze is a version of an old problem. Like the gap between commitments and power
that Lippmann (1943) warned against, or the overstretch Kennedy (1987) traced in great
powers, it arises when obligations outrun the means to meet them; here, though, the
obligations lie in the state's control of its own territory and economy rather than abroad.
Its financial form is overtrading, in which purchases expand f aster than the working
capital needed to fund them, precisely the risk SONASP's buying network runs, while the
state's mines, stakes and purchasing create contingent liabilities that f all on the budget if
gold revenue f ails (Polackova, 1998).

measure, over a twelve-month horizon, is

BCEAO and borrowing is governed by the same rule. The measure also captures only the
financial dimension of capacity; the capacity to operate and secure what the state

3. Context
The gold boom made the strategy affordable. On the LBMA PM fix, gold rose from
US$1,671.75 an ounce when T raoré took power on 30 September 2022 to a peak of
US$5,405 on 29 January 2026, then fell by about a quarter to US$4,001.80 on 25 June and
stayed above US$4,000 in October (SD Bullion, 2022; 2026; PriceGold, 2026). At these
prices control paid. W AF sold gold at US$4,7 44 an ounce in the first half of 2026 against
all-in sustaining costs (AISC) of US$1,823 (W est African Resources, 2026c), and gold
revenue helped cut the budget deficit from 5.8 per cent of GDP in 2024 to 1.8 per cent in
2025 (IMF , 2026b).
The forces moving that price lie f ar from the Sahel. In September 2026, as oil prices driven
up by the war involving Iran fed into US inflation, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates
for the first time in more than three years (The National, 2026). Higher real rates and a
stronger dollar raise the cost of holding gold, and spot gold fell 6.3 per cent that month
(State Street, 2026): a f all consistent with that mechanism, though a single month's
movement cannot isolate its cause.
The monetary setting adds a motive. None of the AES states issues its own currency: the
regional central bank, the BCEAO, sets monetary policy for all eight W AEMU members,
and each government funds its war from revenue and regional borrowing under a shared
deficit rule. T wo of the three have seen that system turned against them. Sanctions in
January 2022 froze Malian state assets in regional banks and led Mali to miss bond
payments (Reuters, 2022), and Niger's assets were frozen after its 2023 coup until
February 2024 (VOA, 2024a). Gold, by contrast, is a store of value a state can hold itself .

dispute with Barrick cut Loulo-Gounkoto's output from 22.5 to 5.5 tonnes in 2025 (Mining
W eekly , 2026a), its state miner holds closed mines (Ecofin, 2025c), and it has no state
buying network comparable to SONASP . Niger took over its one industrial gold mine,
Samira Hill, after output fell to 177 kilograms in 2023 (EWN, 2025). Burkina F aso's scale and

4. The four properties of Burkina Faso's Sovereign Gold Strategy
4.1 Protracted counter-insurgency
The strategy is pursued in a long war over territory where much of Burkina F aso's gold lies.
The IMF , citing the Global T errorism Index, ranks the country second only to P akistan, with
about 2.1 million people displaced, and the government aims at full reconquest of its
territory only by 2030 (IMF , 2026b). The regime took power in 2022 promising to defeat
the insurgency; the war has continued, and in 2024, citing security , it extended its
transition by five years and postponed elections (Al Jazeera, 2022; Africanews, 2024).
Gold is part of what the war is fought over . Armed groups seize or tax artisanal sites
across the central Sahel and smuggle the gold to world markets (International Crisis
Group, 2019), a pattern f amiliar from conflicts in which easily extracted resources finance
armed groups and power is organised around resource areas and trading networks (Le
Billon, 2001). Gold accounts for 90 per cent of recorded mining-related political violence in
the AES states, and JNIM, the main jihadist coalition, taxes local mining and uses some
sites as bases (ACLED , 2026).
The war supplies a motive. T raoré presents drawing gold into official channels as a way to
cut armed groups off from smuggling revenue (Arise News, 2026), and the government
has pledged mining revenue partly to security (North Africa Post, 2025). Whether control
reduces insurgent income is unproven: groups that tax miners at the pit can keep doing so
whoever buys the gold. The war also limits control. Unable to hold the mining areas, the
state concentrates control downstream, at the points gold must pass through to become
money: buying counters in army-held towns, the refinery in Ouagadougou, and the export
channel that the ban on private exports reserves for the state. Where insurgents hold the
resource areas, the state contests the trading networks instead.
4.2 Pragmatic state capitalism
Pragmatic state capitalism is how the strategy integrates vertically along the gold chain,
and two moves stand out: an aggressive campaign against the gold black market, and the
state's emergence as a gold producer . Around them sit the instruments of the 2024
mining code and the decrees that followed. The code raised the state's free carried
interest in every mine from 10 to 15 per cent, gave it a paid subscription right to at least a

Capturing the black market
The campaign against the black market is the core of the strategy . Across Africa, 72 to 80
per cent of artisanal gold goes undeclared, and at least 435 tonnes were smuggled off the
continent in 2022, much of it to the United Arab Emirates (SWISSAID , 2024). In February
2024 Burkina F aso suspended export permits for artisanal and semi-mechanised gold and
directed holders to SONASP (VOA, 2024b), which buys through counters across the
country (IMF , 2026a). Recorded artisanal output rose roughly fivefold, from 8.1 tonnes in
2024 to about 42 tonnes in 2025, and SONASP reported collecting nearly 29 tonnes of
artisanal and semi-mechanised gold in the first half of 2026 (AP A News, 2026a; Capmad,
2026b), roughly the scale of the 26.7 tonnes industrial mines produced (Capmad, 2026d).
The measures are related but not equivalent. Recorded production is a national estimate;
SONASP's collections are its own purchases, which some ministry reports state in raw
rather than fine weight and count as including gold from mining tailings (29.56 tonnes raw ,
or 27.08 tonnes fine, by September 2025; F aso Detox, 2025). The comparison with
industrial output is therefore provisional.
T wo independent sources test these figures. Mirror trade data, which compare what
Burkina F aso reports exporting with what importers report receiving, show the United
Arab Emirates recording about US$265 million more gold from Burkina F aso than Burkina
F aso reported in 2022, and about US$127 million more in 2023: a gap consistent with
smuggling, and narrowing before the ban. In 2024 the UAE's share of Burkina F aso's
officially reported gold exports rose to 35 per cent, from 5 per cent in 2022, while
Switzerland's records match Burkina F aso's to within 2 per cent by value (W orld Bank,
2026; author's calculations). SONASP's 2025 accounts report sales of CF AF 3,281 billion,
about 51 tonnes at the year's average price, broadly consistent with its reported
purchases of artisanal and industrial gold (Horonya Finance, 2026). The collections are
therefore real flows, though whether smuggling has ended cannot be settled until the
UAE reports its 2024 imports. T o the extent the rise reflects formalisation, the state has
captured a flow that once escaped royalties, foreign-exchange repatriation and official
statistics, and the gain expresses all four properties at once: revenue, a blow to insurgent
finance, export earnings channelled into the franc system and a visible act of sovereignty .

near 58 tonnes, which could reflect further formalisation, real growth, reclassified semi-
mechanised output, sales from old stocks or gold drawn from neighbouring countries; the

Becoming a producer
The second move made the state a gold producer in its own right. Through SOP AMIB it
runs Boungou and W ahgnion, transferred in June 2025 after Endeavour Mining's planned
sale of them collapsed (Mining W eekly , 2025b), and is restarting T aparko. The state took
over assets whose owner was leaving, not mines whose operators wanted to stay . In July
2026 SOP AMIB was authorised to build Bouboulou, the first fully state-owned industrial
gold mine, for over CF AF 32 billion (Capmad, 2026c), marking a shift from inheriting mines
to building them; its mines run on rented equipment financed by about CF AF 43 billion in
regional development bank loans over 2025–26 (Capmad, 2026a). The CF AF 11 billion
Raffinor-BF refinery , opened in September 2026, can process 164 tonnes a year and
depends on SONASP for supply (EWN, 2026). Refining to high purity is not the hurdle;
acceptance is. Bars trade freely on the global market only from refineries accredited to
the London Bullion Market Association's Good Delivery standard, and South Africa's Rand
Refinery is the only accredited gold refiner in Africa (Ecofin, 2026e). Until Raffinor-BF is
accredited, domestic refining extends control without giving Burkinabè bars the standing
a reserve asset would need.

Rent capture
W AF shows how the state extracts value where it does not take ownership. It produced
300,383 ounces, about 9.3 tonnes, in 2025 (W est African Resources, 2026d), roughly 18

4.3 Systemic monetary exposure
The strategy rests on a price the state cannot control, and the CF A franc system
determines how a f all must be absorbed. Gold made up nearly 90 per cent of exports in
2025, and without mining revenue the 2025 budget deficit of 1.8 per cent of GDP would
have been 6.0 per cent (IMF , 2026b). Every instrument deepens the exposure: royalties
and dividends f all with the price, gold SONASP holds can lose value before it is sold, and
the largest commitments, from the Kiaka purchase to the state-mine loans, were incurred
during the boom, some through the price peak. The euro peg adds a further channel: gold
is priced in dollars while the franc is fixed at CF AF 655.957 to the euro, so a stronger euro
lowers the franc value of every ounce sold.

the franc's convertibility and credit may be worth more than the freedom to adjust.
4.4 Rhetorical dissonance
The regime frames gold control as the recovery of national wealth from colonial
domination. T raoré said in September 2025 that "the government's approach is to exploit
our gold ourselves" (Ecofin, 2025e); the refinery was presented as keeping mining wealth
at home (Arise News, 2026); and in early 2024 he suggested leaving the franc, promising
to "break all ties that keep us in slavery" (Ecofin, 2024). The conduct is more measured,
and his own words anticipate it: in the same broadcast he said the state would " gradually
take control or co-exploit with companies already operating here" (Ecofin, 2025e). The
request for an extra 35 per cent of Kiaka became a negotiated 25 per cent, and more than
two years after promising to break the ties of "slavery"
, Burkina F aso's finance minister
chairs the monetary union's council of ministers (IMF , 2026b).
The sharpest gap concerns the franc itself . In the statements examined here, the regime
denounces it as a colonial relic without acknowledging what it provides, and the strategy
depends on exactly those benefits: convertibility for SONASP and foreign producers,

regional credit for state mines and the budget, and the stability of pooled reserves and
low inflation (Section 4.3). Admitting any of this would concede that the colonial relic
serves the sovereign project.
The contrast is observable; what follows interprets its function. Read this way , the gap
may be an asset to the strategy , because talk and conduct address different audiences.
The rhetoric is aimed at home, where it plausibly helps secure support for a military
government that has postponed elections (Section 4.1). That support is tied to the war the
regime promised to win, and anti-colonial rhetoric presents gold control both as
recovering national wealth and as cutting armed groups off from revenue. It may also let
negotiated compromise with foreign capital read at home as sovereignty , and channel
anti-colonial demands into gold, where the state can deliver visible results, rather than
into a costly exit from the franc. The conduct is aimed at the miners, lenders, IMF and
BCEAO on which the strategy depends, and on that record it has kept investment, credit
and convertibility . The audiences are not fully separate, however . Financial markets also
hear the talk aimed at home, so the dissonance that sustains support domestically may
raise the strategy's cost of capital abroad by leading investors to overstate the risk of
nationalisation (Section 7.4). That is the trade-off the dissonance carries. That the
dissonance serves the strategy does not show it was designed to.
The franc: an AES test
Does the strategy explain why Burkina F aso keeps the franc? If it did, states with
different approaches to gold should choose differently . They do not: Mali and Niger use
gold differently , have had their assets in the regional system frozen, and still remain
(Section 3). The comparison is not controlled, since the cases also differ in sanctions
exposure, production structure and political chronology , but it is enough to reject gold
strategy as a sufficient or dominant explanation; the general cost of exit for small,
landlocked states at war explains more. A narrower claim survives. Burkina F aso's version,
centred on large-scale purchasing, depends unusually on what the franc provides, so it
raises the cost of leaving without causing the decision to stay .
The sira
The AES's answer to the franc remains on paper . As of September 2026 there was no
confederal central bank, currency or launch date (Rio Times, 2026), Mali had denied that a

economists set an independent central bank and a ban on monetary financing as
preconditions (Ouédraogo, 2026). The obstacles are structural. The credibility problems
of Section 4.3 apply with more force to a currency issued by three governments at war , in
a weak union of poor , landlocked states exposed to the same shocks (Mundell, 1961;
Alesina and Barro, 2002), and leaving the franc would mean dividing the BCEAO's pooled
reserves. Burkina F aso's two-thirds share of AES gold sharpens the problem: a gold-

5. A falling gold price
The four properties meet in a single scenario: a sustained f all in the gold price, which is not
hypothetical after the f alls of 2026 (Section 3). The budget would be hit first. The gap
between the overall and non-mining deficits shows how much the budget relies on gold
(Section 4.3), and the IMF has urged the government to save in good years (IMF , 2026b). A
long f all would reopen much of that gap; the sovereign mining fund, which receives only
revenue above a reference price, would offer little cushion; and, unable to devalue, the
state would have to borrow , tax or cut under the union's deficit limit while security
spending stayed high.
Commitments would not shrink with income. The Kiaka payment, the state-mine loans and
the working capital tied up in purchasing would remain, and SONASP would be most
exposed: it would f ace losses on gold already bought, and any cut in its price or delay in
payment would push gold back to smugglers. W AF shows the mechanism. Its margin over
AISC was about US$2,921 an ounce in the first half of 2026 (W est African Resources,
2026c). On unchanged costs, a f all to US$4,000 would cut revenue per ounce by about 16
per cent and the margin by about 25 per cent; a f all to US$3,500, by 26 and 43 per cent;
and a f all to US$3,000, a level gold first reached in March 2025 (Mining.com, 2025), by 37
and 60 per cent (author's calculations). The state's claims would f all f aster still, since the
royalty rate f alls with the price and income tax and dividends with profits: its fiscal
exposure is highly sensitive to the price. And the cheaper W AF became, the less the state
could afford to buy it, while the Kiaka stake priced in a boom would look expensive.
The squeeze index of Section 2.4 puts numbers on this. At October's price of about
US$4,000 an ounce it stands at about 0.4: the bills of control absorb around two-fifths of
the gold income the fiscal rule leaves free. It reaches 1 after a f all of about a fifth, to
roughly US$3,200, with a range of 16 to 30 per cent across assumptions about loan service,
the timing of the Kiaka payment and the sensitivity of gold income to the price (author's
calculations). The result depends most on whether the deficit ceiling is enforced. If the
government tolerated a deficit of 4 per cent of GDP , the squeeze would bind only after a
f all of 37 to 52 per cent, and the 2024 deficit of 5.8 per cent suggests the ceiling has not
always bound. The war matters almost as much as the price: if security spending widened

5.1 The oil-driven case
The most adverse version is one driven by oil. By September 2026, with the war involving
Iran in its seventh month, oil had again topped US$100 a barrel (The National, 2026), as
attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and the closure of Saudi Arabia's East–W est
pipeline disrupted supply (Reuters, 2026). Oil's main effect on Burkina F aso runs through
the gold price, but only conditionally: inflation alone tends to support gold, which f alls
when central banks push real interest rates up, as the Federal Reserve did in September ,
and rises when they do not, as in the oil shocks of the 1970s.
What makes the oil-driven case worse than any other f all is that costs rise as revenue f alls.
Fuel and fertiliser were 35 per cent of imports in 2025 (IMF , 2026b). The state spent nearly
CF AF 60 billion on fuel subsidies early in 2026, about 0.4 per cent of GDP , and in August
raised the price of diesel for the first time in four years (Benin W eb TV , 2026), while
inflation across the union rose from −0.2 per cent in the first quarter to 1.7 per cent in
August, mainly because of oil (Financial Afrik, 2026; Ecofin, 2026d). The result is a terms-
of-trade squeeze of a kind commodity-dependent African economies know well (Deaton,
1999), with subsidies adding a commitment that grows as gold revenue weakens and a
BCEAO that left its rates unchanged in September (Financial Afrik, 2026). Counting fuel
subsidies at their early-2026 rate alongside the bills of control raises the squeeze index to
about 0.8 at today's price, so that it would bind after a f all of only 1 to 14 per cent, to
between about US$3,450 and US$3,950 (author's calculations). The peg offers only partial
relief: if gold fell 15 per cent while the dollar strengthened 5 per cent, the stronger dollar
would lower the index from about 1.7 to 1.3, but the squeeze would still bind. It is an
adverse scenario rather than an inevitable one, but if escalation and monetary tightening
continue, it is the one the strategy is least equipped to absorb.

6. The Russian pivot
Burkina F aso's break with France has been accompanied by a turn to Russia, but Russia's
role in the strategy should not be overstated. The strategy's drivers are domestic, and the
new gold licences granted to Russian capital are modest: the Niou mine is expected to
yield about 2.5 tonnes a year (AFP , 2024), against national output of about 94 tonnes. The
turn is best read as a patron–client pact between two personalised regimes. Within three
months of the 2022 coup the prime minister visited Russia, in July 2023 T raoré met Putin
in St Petersburg (Kremlin, 2023), and Africa Corps, the state-controlled successor to the
W agner network, arrived later that year (Small W ars Journal, 2026). In personalised
regimes, decisions are concentrated in the ruler and a small circle (Geddes, Wright and
Frantz, 2018), so alliances run through rulers rather than institutions. T raoré gains
protection and a global stage; Putin, isolated after the invasion of Ukraine, gains allies at
low cost and openings in a model that has blended security support with extraction deals
(Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, 2026).
Four kinds of support need distinguishing. The pact provides regime protection, as the
Sahel juntas have used Russian forces to guard against challengers (Small W ars Journal,
2026), and political legitimation, as Russian media amplify their anti-French narratives
(Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, 2026). T erritorial security is doubtful:
Africa Corps has not improved security in contested areas (ADF , 2026). On money , Russia
provides no substitute for the franc's convertibility , regional credit or pooled reserves,
whatever other assistance it may offer . It has become the regime's main security partner
while playing no part in the monetary system on which the strategy depends, which
deepens the dissonance of Section 4.4. In gold it offers an outside option rather than a
new direction. Nordgold, a Russian miner under US sanctions, received the Yimiougou and
Niou permits (Mining W eekly , 2023; AFP , 2024), but the mines minister denied any
national preference (Mining W eekly , 2023); Russian ownership did not protect T aparko,
which Nordgold shut in 2022 (VOA, 2023) and SOP AMIB is restarting; and Burkina F aso
built its own refinery rather than feed the plant Mali is building with Russia's Y adran
Group (BBC, 2025). The pact is also opaque, since what Burkina F aso pays for Russian
security is undisclosed (Mining W eekly , 2023), and fragile: personalised alliances last only
as long as their rulers, and a small client risks abandonment if its patron's priorities shift
(Snyder , 1984). For investors, personalisation means that moves up the escalation ladder
(Section 7.3) can come suddenly .

7.1 Three Australian experiences
Three cases show that risk depends less on nationality than on where an asset sits in the
strategy . W AF , a profitable producer whose operator wants to stay , has f aced rising claims
but not seizure (Section 4.2). Sarama Resources, listed on the ASX and TSX -V , held an
undeveloped asset: in August 2023 its T ankoro 2 exploration permit was rejected nearly
two years after being granted, and the minister then told a Perth mining conference the
permit was available for purchase (Sarama Resources, 2024); Sarama is now claiming
US$242 million in international arbitration (Sarama Resources, 2025). In Mali, showing the
alternative path, the government detained the chief executive of Perth-based Resolute
Mining during tax talks in November 2024 and secured a US$160 million settlement
(Mining W eekly , 2024), and Resolute's shares fell by more than 40 per cent (AAP , 2024).
The pattern is clear: producing operators that stay f ace a rising state share, undeveloped
permits can be withdrawn, and assets whose owners leave pass to the state.
7.2 W AF as an exposure to gold
Investors hold a stock like W AF mainly for alignment with the gold price, and W AF makes
that alignment unusually direct. It is unhedged, selling at prevailing prices (W est African
Resources, 2026b), and its largely fixed costs give it leverage, as the illustration in Section
5 shows. Research on gold-mining equities confirms the pattern: in T uf ano's (1998) study ,
the average North American producer's shares moved about 2 per cent for each 1 per cent
change in the gold price, with exposure lower for firms that hedge and higher for those
with more leverage. Both W AF's mines are in Burkina F aso (W est African Resources,
2026e), so it is a particularly direct ASX -listed exposure to Burkina F aso's gold sector:
holding it means holding unhedged, geared gold and Burkina F aso together . The strategy
weakens that alignment in both directions.
On the upside, the state shares the gains. Royalties rise with the price, from about 8 to 14
per cent of Sanbrado's revenue as gold climbed, and the priority dividend takes a share of
profits. For an unhedged producer , the progressive royalty is the main thing standing
between shareholders and the full spot price: part of the upside a hedge would have sold
to a bank has been claimed by the state. The same feature gives company and state
correlated but asymmetric exposures to the gold price: W AF bears sales, costs and

margins, while the state receives fiscal revenue and carries security , spending and import
obligations. The correlation may help explain why relations have been negotiated rather
than confrontational.
On the downside, the alignment becomes a shared vulnerability . A sustained f all would
cut the state's revenue f aster than W AF's and raise its incentive to extract more just as
margins narrowed: in credit-market terms, wrong-way risk. A hedge book might cushion a
company in exactly that scenario, though how much would depend on its structure and on
the form any intervention took. Unhedged, W AF relies instead on its balance sheet, A$847
million in cash and A$213 million of bullion at the end of March 2026 (W est African
Resources, 2026b), a buffer that cuts both ways: Resolute's initial US$80 million payment
to Mali came from its cash reserves (Mining W eekly , 2024). The result is an asymmetric
exposure to gold, with the upside shared with the state and the downside compounded
by the state's own dependence on the same price.
Insecurity adds a second exposure. A Monte Carlo valuation of W AF's attributable cash
flows, with insecurity modelled as lost production, restart costs and higher security
spending rather than as a discount-rate premium, puts the expected loss at about 6 per
cent of the mines' value under current conditions, equivalent to adding about one
percentage point to the discount rate. W AF's south-central mines have reported no
security incidents, so the base case assumes a 2 per cent annual chance of a major
suspension per mine. At the national rate of security closures, about 6 per cent a year
after 5 to 6 of 17 industrial mines shut in 2021–22 (Mines Actu, 2022; allAfrica, 2023), the
loss rises to about 16 per cent, and at the 2022 peak to about 28 per cent. Whether the
conflict spreads south is therefore the decisive variable, and the risk is skewed: in one
simulation in twenty the loss exceeds 30 per cent (author's calculations).
7.3 Nationalisation risk as an escalation ladder
Nationalisation risk is better framed as an escalation ladder than as a single event, in
Kahn's (1965) sense of a sequence of rungs on which each move is also a bargaining signal.
Fiscal measures sit at the bottom; free equity and paid minority stakes above them;
permit withdrawal and coercive renegotiation under temporary state control higher still;
and permanent takeover at the top. W AF has so f ar been moved up the lower rungs only .
Cumulatively these transfer value to the state through measures none of which takes the
asset, which in economic terms resembles what investment law calls creeping or indirect
expropriation (Dolzer and Schreuer , 2012), though whether any measure meets that legal
standard depends on the applicable treaty , the measure's effects and the investor's
rights.
Even the upper rungs have been used as leverage. Mali seized about three tonnes of gold
from Barrick's Loulo-Gounkoto complex and placed it under provisional administration in
June 2025 (MiningMX, 2025; Canadian Press, 2025), yet the November settlement

returned control to Barrick, which withdrew its arbitration claims (Barrick, 2025). In
Burkina F aso, the legal limit on state participation is unclear . Beyond its 15 per cent free
carried interest, the code gives the state a paid right to subscribe at least a further 30 per
cent, for itself and/or national private investors (Lef aso.net, 2024), and the implementing
decree has been reported as allowing the state and local investors together up to 45 per
cent (Ecofin, 2025f); at Kiaka the state will reach 40 per cent. Whether the state could
take a majority on its own will remain uncertain until the promulgated texts are examined.
What is clear is that any further stake must be paid for , and that cost is the more
immediate constraint. Outright takeover of W AF is unlikely while rent capture delivers a
rising share of its cash flows, SOP AMIB is stretched across four mines, compensation
would have to be financed under the deficit rule and seizure would deter the investment
the strategy depends on.
The risk would rise if W AF tried to exit, as the Boungou and W ahgnion transfers show; if
disputes over taxes or permits escalated, as in Mali; or , most consequentially , if the gold
price fell. Even then the state would most likely climb the middle rungs, through new
levies, pressure over the Kiaka payment and reallocation of idle permits, because a
downturn erodes the capital and capacity a takeover requires; the wrong-way risk of
Section 7.2 would materialise as a climb up the middle of the ladder , not a leap to the top.
The squeeze index gives a sense of its scale: if the state closed the gap entirely by taking

7.4 Is the risk mispriced?
The pattern suggests a hypothesis that follows from rhetorical dissonance. Investors hear
both the rhetoric and the conduct and must decide which to price. If they weight the
rhetoric, they may price ASX -listed producers in Burkina F aso as though the strategy
threatened foreign ownership itself , when its conduct f avours retaining foreign capital
while taking more from it. The dissonance would then create a predictable wedge
between perceived and actual sovereign risk. Resolute's experience shows how sharply
investors react to coercion in the Sahel. T railing multiples cannot test it: in early October
2026, W AF's market capitalisation of about A$4.0 billion was roughly six times trailing
earnings and under three times trailing EBITDA (Kalkine Media, 2026; StockAnalysis,
2026). But such multiples are also what one would expect if investors were pricing
earnings near a cyclical peak, so they are not evidence of mispricing. The security model
offers one benchmark: a country risk premium of 5 to 6 percentage points, common for
high-risk jurisdictions, corresponds to insecurity at the 2022 peak, well above what W AF's
own record suggests (Section 7.2), so applying such a premium on top of modelled losses

8. Causal ambiguities
The framework specifies causal relations among the four properties, but specifying a
mechanism is not identifying it. The paper proceeds by process tracing and structured
comparison (George and Bennett, 2005; Bennett and Checkel, 2015), and its claims differ
in strength. Descriptive claims about the state's instruments and its claims on W AF rest
on primary documents and audited accounts. Interpretive claims, such as the function of
rhetorical dissonance, are inferences from conduct. Causal claims about the links
between the properties are supported by consistent evidence but not isolated from
alternatives. The strongest causal finding is negative: because Mali and Niger keep the
franc despite different gold strategies, gold strategy cannot be the main reason Burkina
F aso stays.
Three sources of ambiguity stand out. The properties reinforce one another in loops, each
partly cause and partly effect, and with all four present in a single country there is no
variation to observe; the counterf actuals of Section 2.2 are reasoned rather than
observed. The gold boom confounds the strategy's apparent success, since a rising state
take and a narrowing deficit cannot be fully separated from what high prices would have
delivered anyway . And measurement is weak: official figures are unverified, recorded
artisanal output may not reflect real change, the effect of gold control on insurgent
revenue is unmeasured, and motives are inferred rather than documented.
Each causal link nonetheless implies something observable. If the war pushes control
downstream, SONASP's collections should track the army's hold on territory , which could
be tested against ACLED's records of violence. If monetary exposure restrains the
rhetoric, talk of leaving the franc should cool as dependence on regional credit grows, and
the record is consistent with this: the 2024 exit talk gave way to chairing the union's
council of ministers and an IMF programme tied to its rules. If the rhetoric drives
commitments, visible acts of control should cluster around moments of political pressure.
And if markets misprice the strategy , W AF's shares should react more to rhetorical events
than to fiscal ones.

The most serious rival explanation is a revenue-maximising state exploiting a boom
(Bremmer and Johnston, 2009). It accounts for the rising take from foreign miners but not
for the concentration of control downstream, the scale of state purchasing or the
decision to stay in a currency the regime denounces. IMF conditionality explains the
transparency commitments but not an export ban or a state buying monopoly; Russian

9. Conclusion
Burkina F aso's Sovereign Gold Strategy has substantially expanded state control over the
formal gold trade since 2022 without transforming that control into monetary sovereignty .
Its character lies in the interaction of protracted counter-insurgency , pragmatic state
capitalism, systemic monetary exposure and rhetorical dissonance, which together have
let the state increase control without wholesale ownership. In the current configuration,
rent capture from foreign producers supplies most of its measurable revenue, artisanal
purchasing carries its largest gross flow , and ownership has so f ar cost comparatively
little while making the state a producer . Russia has become the regime's main security
partner , but not a substitute for the monetary system on which the strategy depends.
Three broader points follow . Resource sovereignty is better understood as control than as
ownership. Controlling a resource suited to money does not by itself produce monetary
sovereignty . And resource sovereignty can deepen a state's practical dependence on a
shared currency: the strategy does not explain why Burkina F aso stays in the franc, but its
reliance on large-scale purchasing makes leaving more costly .
The central vulnerability is the control–capacity squeeze. On the paper's provisional
measure against the gold income the fiscal rule leaves free, the costs of control would
exhaust it after a f all in the gold price of about a fifth, after a f all of only a few per cent in
an oil shock, and with no f all at all if war spending widened the non-mining deficit by a
point of GDP . In state purchasing it would show first as a liquidity strain, as SONASP's thin
margin left gold to drift back to smugglers. Disclosure of the national gold reserve, the
IMF's deadlines for auditing mining permits and publishing mining ownership by the end
of 2026 (IMF , 2026b), and any plan for an AES central bank would test the argument. For
Australian investors, the main risk lies less in nationalisation than in a gradual transfer of
value and an asymmetric exposure to gold. Markets may nonetheless price the rhetoric

rather than the conduct, so the dissonance that sustains the strategy at home may cause
its risks to be misread abroad. Above all, the arrangement has not yet f aced a long f all in
the gold price, which will be its hardest test.
